I missed last year's Looptober, but I'll make a go of it this year. I'm not restricting myself to one single machine this time, anything is fine as long as it's a short piece of loopable music.

My prediction for this year is more live instrumentation and less samples, since I have a nice setup at home with a few synced loop pedals all going through a Zoom LiveTrak L6 which makes for very fast recording.

## Conclusion

I predicted more live instrumentation this year, and that turned out to be correct: Loops one through twenty-two are all based on instruments played live into my loop pedal setup, except for loop seventeen. The last nine loops are made on the Polyend Tracker, except for loop twenty-four which is made directly in Reaper.

Everything is mixed, arranged and "mastered" in Reaper.

## Recording setup

We're already in Nerdville, so I might as well write up my loop recording setup. Perhaps someone reads this and wants to try it out. (You should!) At the very least, if my house burns down, I'll know how to recreate the setup.

It all starts with a Digitech JamMan Solo XT. Let's call it Pedal A. It is connected via sync cable to three Digitech JamMan Express XTs (Pedals B, C and D). The audio is not daisy-chained; every pedal sends to a separate channel on the Zoom LiveTrak L6. 

The JamMan solo goes to channel three on the Zoom, which is set to "double mono", not "stereo". The same goes for channel four. Having them set to mono enables a neat little trick. The guitar is connected to the Vox Stomplab. The Stomplab sends a mono signal to an A/B/Y pedal, and from there a stereo signal goes to the JamMan. This makes it possible to record two completely separated channels on the JamMan. Lay down some guitar on the left channel, then click the A/B/Y pedal, and do an overdub on the right channel. Since the channel is set to double mono on the mixer, you'll get both channels in both ears, which makes is more pleasant than having a chord progression blasting in one ear and a guitar solo screaming in the other. You'll here the channels together, but on the JamMan, they're two separate channels.

So, I connect the guitar or the taishogoto to pedal A. I tap a tempo, then record (usually) a chord progression. Tap the A/B/Y pedal, and record the same chord progression again but with a different setting on the Stomplab. Or I play a melody. Or just noodle around. It doesn't really matter, because it's on a separate channel and I can choose later whether to include it in the mix or not.

I keep the click going, and record the bass onto Pedal B. Pedal B is set up the same way as Pedal A, with an A/B/Y pedal, so any bass flourishes such as slides or octaves go on a separate channel because I suck at bass.

On to Pedal C. I pick a nice kit on the Blackbox and play the requisite amount of bars. This goes to channel five on the mixer, which is a stereo channel.

By now, the groove should be locked in, so I set the click volume to zero. Time to synth.

The OP-1 is connected to Pedal D, which goes to channel six – also a stereo channel. The best strategy would be to just lay down one loop, but usually I can't help myself so I do an overdub or two. This will not be mixable later so I try to get the volume about right on the overdub. Once you start recording, you could always record a few bars and then undo the overdub and keep recording for a few bars. But also remember that challenges are fuel for creativity! I suggest you do multiple overdubs and deal with it later.

By now you should have a nice beat going. Time to move to channel one. That's the mic channel. I have a turtle neck mic stand attached to the stand for the Drumbeam. It holds a Shure 565 Unisphere because that's the best mic in the world. I usually have a good amount of reverb on channel one. The effects don't get recorded anyway, so it's just to get the right feeling. Depending on the loop, I pick up the saxophone or the harmonica or maybe some pots and pans and a wooden spoon. Then I go to town.

But first, I hit record on the Zoom. And remember: It's always good to record a little more stuff than you think you'll need. Perhaps play the same figure in two octaves, or try some different melodies. Make a weird sound with your mouth. Clap your hands or snap your fingers. You will thank yourself later while you're arranging. 

After a couple of minutes of making sounds, I stop the recording, plug in my computer and transfer the stems. I open up Chubby Click on my phone and tap the tempo so I know what tempo to set in Reaper. Then I hit the main switch on the power strip, turning everything off. It's a really pleasant feeling. Everything you did the last ten minutes is gone, except for the files on your computer.

I start a new project in Reaper, set the tempo, and drag in the stems. The tempo is usually off by a few cents, so I chop all the stems at the loop point and time stretch them to fit the grid. Then I go through track one to see if there's anything worth using. Sometimes I fix timing issues on the other tracks, but I try to keep it as untouched as I can bear listening to. If I wanted perfection, I would have programmed it all.

That's about it. You might be wondering: What about channel two? At the moment, channel two is the return channel for effect send. The Zoom actually has two effect sends, and I've set up send two to go to a Polytune tuner. It lets me tune any instrument quickly, and doesn't need a return. Send one goes through a Nux Duotime and an Earthquaker Rainbow Machine. I don't use it much, but it's nice to have if I want to get weird. All channels on the Zoom gets recorded automatically anyway so I usually listen to it and if there's something there, I keep it. In the future, I might put my backup JamMan XT on channel two and run an extra mic through it. That would allow me to have for example a saxophone bit be part of the loop, which would be nice if I ever decide to take this Spaceage Busker setup on the road.

Another thing that would be nice for live purposes is a dedicated mute pedal. I've actually started building this, but it's not done yet. The Zoom has midi in, and you can mute/unmute a channel by sending the correct CC code. You could of course just stop each individual pedal if you want it to be silent for a while, but it only starts up again at the next loop point. So for on-the-fly arranging, it's more precise to be able to mute the channel with a stomp box sending midi cc data. The stomp box I'm building is designed to have two modes. In normal mode, you tap the channel button once to mute, and then again to unmute. In mode two, the channel is muted by default, and unmuted momentarily when you tap or hold the button, for stutter effects.
